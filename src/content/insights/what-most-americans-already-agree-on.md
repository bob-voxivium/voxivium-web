The story of American politics is usually told as a story of division. On the questions that dominate coverage, that is accurate. But it is not the whole picture, and the part left out is large.

There is a set of questions where agreement runs above 80 percent, and where the partisan gap nearly disappears. These are not obscure issues. Several concern how Congress itself operates. They get little attention precisely because there is no fight to cover.

Here are six, with the numbers.

## 1. Barring members of Congress from trading individual stocks

**86 percent support.** Republicans 87 percent, Democrats 88 percent, independents 81 percent.

The Program for Public Consultation at the University of Maryland surveyed 2,625 registered voters, first giving each respondent a briefing on the proposal and the strongest arguments on both sides, reviewed by proponents and opponents for accuracy. Support held after respondents heard the case against.

Party affiliation moved the number by a single point between Republicans and Democrats.

## 2. Term limits for members of Congress

**87 percent favor.** 56 percent strongly favor. 12 percent oppose.

Pew Research Center found in September 2023 that support for limiting congressional terms crosses every demographic line it measured. Separate work by the Program for Public Consultation put Republican support at 86 percent, Democratic at 80 percent, and independent at 84 percent.

This one carries a real obstacle: it would almost certainly require a constitutional amendment, which needs two-thirds of both chambers and ratification by 38 states. Popularity and feasibility are different things.

## 3. Paper backups for electronic voting machines

**82 percent favor.**

Pew surveyed 5,140 adults in January 2024. The striking part is not the topline but the internals: conservative Republicans favored it at 88 percent and liberal Democrats at 86 percent. On an election-administration question, in an election year, the two groups furthest apart landed two points from each other.

## 4. Letting Medicare negotiate prescription drug prices

**85 percent support.** Democrats 90 percent, independents 87 percent, Republicans 79 percent.

KFF has tracked this for years and the number stays high. It also survives argument: when KFF presented respondents with the pharmaceutical industry's case against negotiation, majority support held.

## 5. Holding federal judges to the same ethics rules as lawmakers

**Democrats 91 percent, independents 86 percent, Republicans 78 percent.**

A March 2025 Fix the Court poll asked whether judges and justices should follow the same ethics rules that apply to members of Congress. Every group agreed by wide margins. This is notable because judicial questions are usually where partisan splits are sharpest.

## 6. An independent audit of all federal spending

**89 percent support.** Republicans 99 percent, independents 89 percent, Democrats 83 percent.

Cato Institute polling found near-unanimity among Republicans and strong majorities elsewhere for auditing federal spending to identify waste and fraud. The 99 percent figure is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly: it is close to the ceiling of what any survey question reaches.

## What to make of this

Two honest caveats.

First, agreement on a goal is not agreement on a method. Ninety percent of people can want federal spending audited and still disagree completely about who should run the audit, what counts as waste, and what to do with the findings. Polls capture direction, not detail.

Second, popular does not mean correct, and it does not mean easy. Term limits poll near universally and remain constitutionally difficult. A high number is a fact about public opinion, not a verdict on the policy.

With those caveats, the pattern holds. On a real set of questions, most Americans want the same thing, and many of them do not know it. Coverage selects for conflict, which means consensus is systematically underreported. People end up believing the other side disagrees with them about far more than it does.

That gap between actual agreement and perceived agreement is worth closing. It is the reason Voxivium measures what constituents believe and reports it back in aggregate, rather than amplifying whichever position is loudest.
