Any platform that grades elected officials owes its users an account of what its grade is built from and where that foundation is weak. This is ours.

Voxivium scores representatives primarily against their recorded votes. We think that is the right primary source. We also think it has real limits, and a scoring system that hides those limits is doing something closer to marketing than measurement.

## Why votes are the right foundation

Three properties make roll call votes unusually good evidence.

They are **binding**. A vote changes law or fails to. Unlike a speech or a press release, it has a consequence attached, which means it costs something to cast.

They are **complete and public**. Every recorded vote in both chambers is published by the government itself, name by name. There is no sampling, no interpretation, and no gatekeeper deciding which votes count.

They are **uniform**. Every member of a chamber faces the same question at the same moment. That makes comparison between members meaningful in a way that comparing rhetoric never is.

Most accountability signals fail at least one of these. Campaign statements are not binding. Committee work is not uniform. Media appearances are not complete. Votes clear all three.

## Where votes fall short

### Procedural votes look like policy votes

A large fraction of recorded votes decide process rather than substance: whether to end debate, whether to table an amendment, whether to proceed to consideration at all.

A member can vote to advance a bill they plan to oppose, so it gets a full debate. Another can block a bill they largely support because one provision is unacceptable. Both appear in the record as a single Yea or Nay with no indication of which kind of decision it was.

Any system that treats every vote as a straightforward statement of policy preference will misread a meaningful share of them.

### Bills bundle unrelated provisions

Legislation is frequently packaged. One vote may cover disaster relief, an unrelated funding adjustment, and a policy rider that has nothing to do with either.

When a member votes Nay, the record shows opposition to the package. It cannot show which component drove the decision. Scoring that vote against any single issue in the bundle involves an inference, and that inference is sometimes wrong.

### Absence is ambiguous

"Not Voting" appears in the record without explanation. It covers illness, travel, family emergency, and the deliberate choice to avoid a politically costly vote. These are very different behaviors that produce identical entries.

We do not count absences as positions. Treating them as opposition would penalize a member for a hospital stay; treating them as support would reward strategic avoidance. Neither is defensible, so absences are excluded rather than guessed at.

### Silence is invisible

Most bills never receive a recorded vote. They are introduced, referred to committee, and stop. A committee chair who prevents a bill from advancing has exercised more influence over the outcome than most members who eventually vote on it, and the vote record shows none of that.

By the same token, a member who never brings forward legislation on an issue leaves no trace in the record. Absence of a vote is not evidence of a position, and it is not evidence of the absence of one.

## How we handle this

Four commitments follow from the above.

**Every score links to its underlying votes.** A grade you cannot audit is a claim you have to take on faith. Each component of a Voxivium score traces to specific roll calls, viewable individually.

**Issue mapping is published and correctable.** Deciding that a given vote bears on a given issue is a judgment, not a fact. We publish those mappings, and when a representative or a user shows one to be wrong, we fix it. The underlying tally is not disputable; our categorization of it is.

**Patterns outweigh single votes.** One vote on a bundled bill carries limited information. Twenty votes in the same policy area carry a great deal. Scores weight accordingly.

**Coverage is stated, not hidden.** Where we have thin evidence on an issue, the interface says so rather than presenting a confident grade built on two data points. A number without its sample size is not a measurement.

## The honest summary

A voting record is the strongest available evidence of what an elected official actually did, and it is incomplete. Both statements are true at once.

The correct response is not to abandon the measurement, and not to oversell it. It is to publish the method, show the inputs, mark the gaps, and let people check the work. That is the standard we hold ourselves to, and the one worth applying to any accountability score you encounter, including ours.
