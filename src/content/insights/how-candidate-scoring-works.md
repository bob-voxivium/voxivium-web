Sitting members of Congress have voting records. Challengers do not. For a first-time candidate there is no roll call history to grade, which leaves most voters comparing campaign slogans and hoping for the best.

Voxivium handles candidates differently from incumbents. This is how the candidate score is built, and what it can and cannot tell you.

## Step one: you say what you believe

Before any candidate is scored, you answer for yourself.

Voxivium organizes political belief into 20 domains, grouped under six broad categories. Each domain has one high-level **anchor** question that captures the underlying value, plus a set of specific **policy** questions beneath it. There are just over 100 policy questions in total.

Two things get recorded for every answer. Where you stand, on a scale running from one pole to the other rather than a yes-or-no. And how much you care, which matters as much as the position itself.

The importance weighting is what keeps the score yours. Two people can hold identical positions across all 20 domains and still get different scores for the same candidate, because they weight those domains differently. A score built without that weighting is measuring the average voter, who does not exist.

For candidate scoring, the 20 anchor questions do the work. Policy-level questions require a legislative record to match against, and challengers do not have one.

## Step two: we read what the campaign published

For each candidate, Voxivium collects the public text of their campaign website and works out where it places them on those same 20 domains.

Three rules govern this.

**Only the candidate's own words count.** Campaign site text, published platform documents, official position statements. Not news coverage, not opposition research, not social media commentary about them.

**Every position must carry receipts.** When the system concludes a candidate holds a position on a domain, it captures one to three passages from their own material that support that reading. Those passages are shown to you with a link to the source. You can check whether the interpretation is fair, and disagree with it if it is not.

**Silence stays silence.** If a campaign says nothing about a domain, the candidate is not assigned a position on it. No inference from party, no inference from adjacent issues, no filling in the blanks.

That last rule is the one that shapes everything else.

## Step three: two numbers, not one

A single letter grade would hide the most important thing about a campaign platform, so Voxivium reports two separate figures.

**Match rating** is how closely the candidate lines up with you, calculated only across the domains where they have actually taken a position. For each of those domains, alignment is the distance between your stance and theirs, and each domain contributes in proportion to how important you said it is.

**Platform clarity** is how much of the map the candidate has filled in: the share of your 20 domains where their published material supports any position at all.

Reading them together is the point.

A candidate at 85 percent match and 70 percent clarity has addressed most of what matters and largely agrees with you. A candidate at 85 percent match and 20 percent clarity agrees with you on the four things they have talked about, and is a blank page on the other sixteen. Those are very different candidates, and a single blended number would present them as nearly the same.

The interface splits the difference visually: domains where the candidate has stated a position, ranked by alignment, and then a separate group for the ones where they have said nothing.

## What silence actually means

An unaddressed domain is genuinely ambiguous, and worth being careful about.

Sometimes it means the issue is outside the office. A candidate for a seat with no jurisdiction over a subject has a reasonable basis for not publishing a position on it.

Sometimes it means strategic omission. Campaigns make deliberate choices about which issues to foreground, and a position that would cost more votes than it gains is a common thing to leave out. On a genuinely contested issue in a race where it is live, silence is a choice.

Sometimes it means the campaign is small. A well-funded operation publishes a detailed platform because it can afford staff to write one. A first-time candidate running on their own time may have four paragraphs and a donate button. That is a fact about resources, not about candor.

Voxivium does not try to guess which of these applies. It reports the gap and shows you which domains are affected, weighted by how much you said those domains matter. A blank on something you rated critically important is a very different thing from a blank on something you rated marginal, and the display reflects that.

What we will not do is convert silence into a position and then grade it.

## Should you trust a candidate with no website at all?

This question comes up often enough to answer directly, and the honest answer is more careful than the intuitive one.

Absence of a platform is not evidence of bad character. Down-ballot races are frequently contested by people with day jobs and no campaign staff. Many local offices are won by candidates whose entire public presence is a name on a ballot. Treating that as disqualifying would systematically favor whoever raised the most money, which is the opposite of what an accountability tool should do.

What absence of a platform does mean is that you have no basis for prediction. You cannot know whether someone will represent your views if they have never stated theirs. That is a real cost, and it should be weighed as one.

The distinction worth drawing is between a candidate who has not published a platform and one who has published a platform that avoids the contested questions. The first is often a resource constraint. The second is a decision, made by people who thought carefully about which issues to skip.

Voxivium shows you which it is, by showing you exactly which domains are covered and which are not.

## When a candidate is also a Voxivium user

Candidates who join Voxivium answer the same belief questions every voter does. If they choose to make those answers public, their stated beliefs appear alongside what their campaign material says.

This is useful in both directions. It closes gaps where the campaign site is silent, and more importantly it lets you see whether a candidate's campaign positions match the positions they record privately. When both are present, campaign material carries the greater weight, since a public platform is a public commitment in a way an in-app answer is not.

Making beliefs public is entirely the candidate's choice, and choosing not to is not held against them in the score.

## The limits

Three worth stating plainly.

Campaign text is a snapshot. Platforms change, and a score reflects the material as of when it was read.

Reading intent from prose involves judgment. A campaign might write a paragraph that could reasonably be read two ways. This is exactly why the supporting passages are shown rather than summarized: so you can apply your own reading rather than trusting ours.

Stated positions are not conduct. A challenger's score predicts nothing about what they will actually do in office. It measures what they have committed to publicly. Once they have a voting record, that record takes over, and the two can be compared.

That comparison is the entire point of building it this way. A candidate score is a promise. A voting record is a result. Voxivium is designed to hold both, so that the distance between them becomes visible.
