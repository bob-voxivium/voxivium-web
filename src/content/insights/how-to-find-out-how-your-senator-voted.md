Every recorded vote in Congress is public. Not summarized, not filtered through a press release: the actual tally, with every member's name next to how they voted. It is free to look up, and it takes about two minutes once you know where to go.

Most people never do, because the official sites are built for congressional staff rather than for citizens. This guide walks through the process.

## Start with the chamber, not the person

There is no single "how did my representative vote" page on any government site. Records are organized by chamber and by vote, not by member. So the first question is which chamber you care about.

**For senators**, go to the Senate's [roll call votes page](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/votes.htm). Votes are grouped by Congress and session. Pick the year, and you get a numbered list of every recorded vote with a one-line description of what was being decided.

**For representatives**, go to the [Clerk of the House votes page](https://clerk.house.gov/Votes). Same structure: choose the year, get the list.

Click any vote and you land on the full tally. Every member is listed by name with a Yea, Nay, Present, or Not Voting beside them. Find your member alphabetically.

## Search by bill when you know the topic

Working backward from a vote number only helps if you already know which vote you want. More often you know the subject.

For that, use [Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/), the Library of Congress's official legislative database. Search a topic or a bill number, open the bill, and check the "Actions" tab. Any recorded votes appear there with links to the tallies.

This is also where you see the parts that never reach a vote. A bill's action history shows when it was introduced, which committee received it, and whether it moved. Most bills die in committee without a recorded vote, which is a meaningful fact about a legislative agenda that vote tallies alone will not show you.

## Third-party trackers are easier to read

[GovTrack](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/votes) reorganizes the same public data around the member rather than the vote. You can pull up a single legislator and scroll their voting history in one place, which the official sites do not offer.

The underlying records are identical. The difference is presentation, and for a citizen rather than a staffer, that difference is most of the work.

## What the record does not tell you

A roll call vote is a fact. What it means usually requires context the tally does not include.

**Procedural votes look like policy votes.** A large share of recorded votes are on process: whether to end debate, whether to table an amendment, whether to advance a bill to consideration. A member can vote to advance a bill they intend to vote against, or block one they broadly support because of a specific provision. The tally says only Yea or Nay.

**Bills bundle unrelated things together.** A single vote can cover disaster relief, an unrelated funding change, and a policy rider. A Nay tells you the member opposed the package. It does not tell you which part.

**Absence is ambiguous.** "Not Voting" covers illness, travel, family emergencies, and deliberate avoidance of a difficult vote. The record does not distinguish between them.

**Voice votes leave no record at all.** When a measure passes by voice vote or unanimous consent, there is no tally, and no individual positions to look up.

None of this makes voting records unreliable. They remain the most direct evidence available of what an elected official actually did, as opposed to what they said. But a single vote is a data point, not a verdict. Patterns across many votes carry far more signal than any one of them.

## The practical problem

Doing this well for one bill is easy. Doing it across a full term, for two senators and a representative, comparing the pattern against what you actually believe, is a research project most people will never take on.

That gap is the reason Voxivium exists. The records are public; the work of turning them into an answer to "does this person vote the way I would" is what has been missing.
